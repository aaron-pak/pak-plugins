Hypothesis tests, on one page

The idea, the five steps, one worked example, the two ways to be wrong, and cards to test yourself the night before.

The idea in one sentence

A p-value is how surprising your data would be if nothing were going on.

The routine

Five steps, the same every time

Worked example

Is the coin fair? 62 heads in 100 flips

If the coin is fair we expect 50 heads, give or take 5 (the standard deviation is √(100 × 0.5 × 0.5) = 5). We got 62, which is 2.4 standard deviations above.

z = (62 − 50) ÷ 5 = 2.4p ≈ 0.02 both tails

At α = 0.05 that is surprising enough: reject “the coin is fair”. The exact count from the binomial gives p = 0.021, close to the shortcut.

Heads in 100 fair flips, % of the time

Clay: as far from 50 as our 62, or further. Each of those bars is under half a percent; with the rest of the tails they add up to 2.1%.

Two ways to be wrong

What you decide against what's true

α is the false alarm rate you choose. Power is the chance of catching a real effect, usually aimed at 80%.

Keep “nothing going on”Reject it

Nothing going onA real effect

Right

Nothing was going on, and you said so.

Type I error

A false alarm

You reject, but nothing was going on. Happens α of the time.

Type II error

A miss

There was an effect and you didn't see it. Happens β of the time.

Right, and the point

You find the real effect. This is power, 1 − β.

Check yourself

A test gives p = 0.03. Which of these is true?

Answers
Right

The p-value is about the data, given the null. It says nothing about how big the effect is.

Not quite

The p-value assumes the null is true, so it can't also be the chance that it is. And a tiny effect can have a small p with enough data.
